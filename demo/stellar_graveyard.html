The Stellar Graveyard

Chapter 1

What is a gravitational wave?

Anything with mass bends space-time, the fabric of space and time. When two very heavy objects whirl around each other, they stir it up and send out ripples called gravitational waves. The ripples carry energy away, so the pair slowly spirals closer, orbits faster, and finally collides.

By the time the ripples reach Earth they are unbelievably small. The LIGO detectors notice their 4-kilometre arms changing length by less than a thousandth of the width of a proton.

As the objects get closer, the waves rise in pitch and get louder, so if you turn them into sound you hear a chirp. Build your own merger below. Pick two masses and compare a light pair with a heavy one.

Merger simulator

Try a real one:

This simulator uses the simplest version of the chirp formula: Newton's gravity plus the energy the waves carry away. It gets the timing and pitch roughly right, but it is an illustration, not real data, and it stops at the moment of merger. The final mass uses a rule of thumb taken from the real catalog: the leftover black hole is usually about 96% of the two masses added together.

How do astronomers get masses from a sound?

The shape of the chirp gives them away. How quickly the pitch rises tells you the chirp mass, and the pitch at the moment of merger tells you the total mass. Heavy pairs merge at a low pitch, after only a few wiggles. Light pairs, like two neutron stars, hum for minutes and climb to a high squeal. Comparing how loud the signal is with how loud it would be up close tells you the distance.

Every number in the catalog is a best guess (the median) plus a 90% range, because noise blurs the signal.

Chapter 3

Which masses are common, and which are missing?

Sorting the masses into bins shows which ones nature makes most often, and which it seems to avoid. The bins get wider as the masses grow (a logarithmic scale), so 1-Sun neutron stars and 100-Sun black holes fit on one chart. The gray bands mark two ranges where astronomers expected to find almost nothing: the lower mass gap and the pair-instability gap.

What to notice

  • Two crowds. Black holes pile up around 10 and around 35 times the Sun's mass. Those are the most common black holes that end up merging.
  • The lower gap really is quiet. Only one object, in GW230529, sits clearly between 3 and 5 Suns.
  • The pair-instability gap is not empty. 52 heavier black holes sit between 50 and 130 Suns. Switch to "Black hole left after" to see why that's less surprising for leftovers: two 30-Sun black holes make a 57-Sun one.
  • Careful: heavy mergers are louder, so the catalog has more of them than a fair sample of the universe would. That's a selection effect.

Chapter 5

How far can we hear?

Gravitational waves travel at the speed of light, so looking far away also means looking back in time. The farthest merger here happened more than 8 billion years ago, before the Sun existed. Each dot shows the black hole left over after a merger, plotted against its distance or its light-travel time.

Across

The black bars show the middle value (median) of the final mass for the nearest, middle and farthest thirds of the mergers.

The farthest third of the mergers are more than three times heavier than the nearest third. Black holes weren't heavier in the past. Heavy mergers are louder, so we can hear them from much farther away, while small ones are only heard when they are close. Astronomers call this a selection effect, and correct for it before saying what the universe is really like.

Chapter 6 · Stretch goal

The forbidden zone

The cores of the very biggest stars get so hot that light turns into pairs of electrons and positrons. That drains the pressure holding the core up, so it collapses and sets off a runaway nuclear explosion. The heaviest stars blow themselves apart completely and leave no black hole at all. So theory says that between roughly 50 and 130 Suns, no black hole can be born straight from a star: the pair-instability gap.

Yet the catalog has black holes there. One explanation: they are second-generation black holes, built by earlier mergers. Those tend to spin, so spin (the dot colors) is a clue. Nobody knows exactly where the gap starts, so drag the slider and see how the answer changes.

50 M☉

Each row is one merger whose heavier black hole is very likely above 40 Suns. The line is its 90% range and the dot is the best guess, colored by effective spin. Rows in solid ink have their whole range inside or above the gap. Faded rows might be below it.

Heavy black holes give very short signals, so their spins are hard to measure, and 19 events is a small sample. The spins are a hint that some gap black holes were built by earlier mergers, not proof.
